Can You Enter an Onsen With Tattoos in Japan? How to Find a Bath That Says Yes

There is no national rule — each facility decides for itself, and most never publish their policy. Here is how to find a published “yes,” how to ask the rest in Japanese before you travel, and how to book a private bath so the question never comes up.

You have booked the ryokan, pictured the steam rising off the outdoor bath, and then the doubt arrives: will they even let me in? You check the facility’s website. It says nothing about tattoos at all — no ban, no welcome, just silence. Forums contradict each other. One traveler was waved in with a full sleeve; another was turned away at the door for a small ankle piece.

That silence is the normal case in Japan, not an exception. No honest guide can promise you a yes, because each facility decides for itself. What actually works is a skill: finding baths that welcome tattoos, asking the rest in writing before you travel, and knowing the private-bath fallback. This guide walks through all three.

The short answer: no national rule, either way

There is no national tattoo ban, and there is no national right to bathe either. Each facility sets its own policy.

The only nationwide government count is old, and you should read it that way. In the Japan Tourism Agency’s October 2015 survey, 581 hotels and ryokan with baths replied: about 56% said they refused bathers with tattoos, about 31% said they did not refuse them, and about 13% allowed them under conditions such as covering the tattoo with a sticker. In March 2016 the agency told industry associations that refusing someone solely because of a tattoo is not appropriate, and suggested cover stickers, quieter time slots and private baths as alternatives. That was guidance, not law — facilities still decide.

One more fact explains the silence you found online: most facilities simply never publish a policy. When one bath directory checked 6,173 listings, about 77% had no tattoo policy stated at all. That is why asking well — not guessing — is the core skill here.

Step 1: Know which kind of bath you are dealing with

  • Ryokan and hotel baths. The most cautious category, and the one the 2015 survey covered. A policy for the inn’s large shared bath says nothing about its private baths — ask about both separately.
  • Day-use onsen and super sento (large leisure bath complexes). Rules vary widely. Many publish a clear tattoo policy on their website or at the entrance, precisely because they get asked often. If a super sento sells cover stickers at the front desk, that is usually a sign covered tattoos are accepted — confirm before you pay.
  • Neighborhood sento (public bathhouses). Often the most relaxed option, and usually the cheapest. The trade-off: a sento is a working local bath, not a spa. Soap, shampoo and towels are frequently not included — you bring your own or buy/rent them at the counter for a small fee.

Step 2: Search for a published “yes” first

Start where the answer is already written down. Search the facility name plus タトゥーOK or 入れ墨OK (“tattoos OK”), and check tattoo-friendly listings from official tourism sites such as the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) and local visitor bureaus. Read the exact wording: “Tattoos allowed if covered” is not the same as “tattoos welcome,” and a sign reading 入れ墨・タトゥーお断り means tattoos are refused.

Two towns are the standard examples, as checked on November 21, 2026 — policies can change, so re-confirm before a trip built around them:

  • Kinosaki Onsen (Hyogo): the official Visit Kinosaki site states that all seven public bathhouses (soto-yu) allow tattoos of all sizes, with no need to cover them. Baths inside individual inns may differ.
  • Kusatsu Onsen (Gunma): the operator’s FAQ for its three public baths — Goza-no-yu, Otaki-no-yu and Sai-no-kawara Rotenburo — states that it does not specifically refuse bathers with tattoos, and asks guests to follow bathing manners. Note the difference: Kinosaki publishes a welcome; Kusatsu publishes a non-refusal for three named baths, not the whole town.

Step 3: If the policy is not published, ask — in Japanese, in writing

Silence is not a yes, and it is not a no. Email or message the facility before you book. Written Japanese gets a considered answer; a nervous question at the front desk, in English, often gets the safest answer, which is no.

Asking about the shared bath:

タトゥーがあるのですが、入浴できますか？カバーシールで隠せば入浴できますか？
“I have a tattoo. May I use the bath? May I use it if I cover the tattoo with a cover sticker?”

Asking about a private bath instead:

タトゥーがあるのですが、貸切風呂はありますか？予約方法と料金を教えてください。
“I have a tattoo. Do you have a private bath? Please tell me how to reserve it and the price.”

Send a photo or the size of the tattoo if asked — “about the size of my palm” （手のひら大） is a useful, concrete phrase. Keep the reply: a written yes is your reference if a different staff member is on duty when you arrive. At the front desk, you can also show: タトゥーがあります。入浴できますか？ (“I have a tattoo. May I use the bath?”)

Step 4: Book a private bath — the route that removes the question

A kashikiri-buro is a bath your group rents for a set time, usually 45–60 minutes. Because nobody else shares the water, tattoo policies for the shared bath generally do not apply — confirm that one point when you reserve. As a price guide, expect roughly ¥2,000–5,000 per hour at many ryokan and day-use facilities, sometimes free for staying guests, sometimes charged on top of admission; treat this as a planning range, not a quote.

At a ryokan, private baths are often reserved at check-in, in time-slot order — the popular evening slots go first, so ask at the front desk before you unpack. Some facilities take reservations in advance; day-use facilities may be first-come, first-served with a sign-up sheet. On the day, arrive a few minutes before your slot and vacate on time — and wash before soaking, as in any bath.

Step 5: Cover stickers — useful, small, and never assumed

Cover stickers are skin-tone adhesive sheets sold online, at some drugstores and variety stores, and occasionally at bath front desks. As a rough guide, common sheets run from postcard-size up to about A4, and one small-to-medium tattoo per sheet is the realistic plan. Three limits matter: the facility must allow covered entry in the first place, a large piece, back piece or full sleeve will not disappear under a sheet, and stickers can lift at the edges in hot water — bring a spare. Never treat a sticker as a way to sneak past a stated ban; if staff notice, you will be asked to leave the bath.

FAQ

The website says nothing about tattoos. Can I just go?

Silence means “ask,” nothing more — about 77% of listings in one large directory state no policy at all. Asking by email a week early leaves you with a written yes, or time to book somewhere else.

If Kinosaki welcomes tattoos, is every bath there fine?

The published welcome covers the town’s seven public bathhouses. An individual inn’s own bath on the same street may set a different policy — check it separately.

Will a cover sticker work for a large tattoo?

Realistically, no. A sheet handles a small-to-medium piece, and only where the facility has confirmed covered entry in advance.

What does a private bath cost?

Typically roughly ¥2,000–5,000 per hour for a 45–60 minute slot, though some ryokan include it free for staying guests and premium resorts charge more. Confirm the price and slot when you reserve.
