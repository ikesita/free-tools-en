Japan Resident Tax: How to Read the June Bill (Juminzei Notice) Field by Field

It arrives in your second June in Japan, it is based on money you earned last year, and nothing about your current salary explains it. Here is how to decode the notice, check it against your own payslips, and pay the right amount by the right date.

It is your second June in Japan. An official-looking envelope arrives from your city or ward office — or, if you have just changed jobs, a set of payment slips lands in your mailbox with a total that looks like a month’s rent. Nothing about your salary changed. Nobody warned you. Your first three questions are the ones everyone asks: is this a mistake, how much is this really, and when exactly do I have to pay it?

It is almost certainly not a mistake. It is resident tax (住民税, jūminzei), the local tax that funds the city and prefecture you live in. The confusion comes from its timing: the tax is always one year behind your income. Once you understand that, the rest of the notice falls into place.

The short answer

  • Resident tax is charged on last year’s income. The tax that starts this June is based on what you earned from January to December of the previous year. That is why there was nothing to pay in your first year — and why the bill feels sudden in your second.
  • Who charges you is fixed on January 1. You owe the tax to the municipality where your address was registered on January 1 of this year, even if you have moved since.
  • Employees usually pay through payroll. Under special collection (特別徴収), your employer deducts the tax in 12 monthly instalments from June to the following May.
  • If nobody is deducting it, you pay by slips. Under ordinary collection (普通徴収) — self-employment, a gap between jobs, a handover that did not happen — you pay yourself, typically in four instalments due around the end of June, August, October, and the following January. The dates printed on your slips are the ones that count.

First, identify which document you are holding

Employees receive a tax amount decision notice (特別徴収税額決定通知書), usually via their employer around May or June. It states your annual tax and the monthly deduction for each month from June to May. People paying by ordinary collection receive a tax notice with payment slips (納税通知書 and 納付書) directly from the municipality, typically in June. The slips are the part you actually pay with; the notice is the part that explains the amount. Keep both: the notice is your only breakdown of how the amount was calculated. Layouts differ by city, but the core fields below appear almost everywhere.

The tax base and the two parts: 課税標準, 所得割, 均等割

Start with 課税標準 (kazei hyōjun), the “tax base” — the amount of income the tax is actually charged on. It is not your salary. Your salary has already passed through the employment income deduction and any other deductions you qualify for, such as social insurance premiums you paid, the basic deduction, and dependent deductions, which are listed in the deductions section of the notice. If the income figure at the top does not match last year’s earnings, check it against your withholding slip (源泉徴収票, gensen chōshūhyō) from last year’s employer — that document is the starting point for the whole calculation.

Your total has two components. 所得割 (shotokuwari) is the income-based part: the tax rate applied to your tax base. In most municipalities the combined standard rate, municipal plus prefectural, is around 10%, though small local variations exist. 均等割 (kintōwari) is the flat per-capita part: everyone who owes resident tax pays the same fixed annual amount, regardless of income. It is why even a low-income year rarely produces a bill of exactly zero. You may also see adjustment lines, such as credits for housing loans or donations, including furusato nōzei. The final annual amount, often labelled 年税額, is the number everything else is built from.

How you pay: special collection, ordinary collection, and the job-change trap

Look for the collection method on the notice. 特別徴収 (tokubetsu chōshū) means your employer deducts it: check that the monthly amounts on the notice match the resident tax line on your payslips from June onwards. 普通徴収 (futsū chōshū) means you pay the slips yourself, at banks, post offices, convenience stores within the amount limit printed on the slip, or via payment methods your municipality supports, such as smartphone payment apps or the national local-tax payment website — check the back of the slip for the options that apply in your city.

If you have just changed jobs, this section matters most. The balance from your old employer’s deductions does not automatically follow you. Until your new employer’s special collection starts, the remaining amount is often switched to ordinary collection — the same annual tax, collected a different way; a job change never cancels it. Each slip carries its own period number (第1期, 第2期, and so on) and deadline (納期, nōki). You can usually pay the full year at once using the first slip set. Keep the stamped receipt (領収書) — you may be asked for proof of tax payment.

If you cannot pay on time, go before the deadline

Do not ignore the envelope: late-payment charges can accrue, and reminders follow. Municipalities generally offer a consultation at the tax payment counter (納税相談) if you contact them before the deadline, and paying in smaller instalments (分納, bunnō) may be possible. Bring the notice, an ID, and a realistic idea of what you can pay and when. Two phrases cover most visits: Jūminzei no nōfūsho desu — “These are my resident tax payment slips” — and Bunnō no sōdan o shitai desu — “I would like to ask about paying in instalments.”

FAQ

My salary has not changed, so why did this bill appear?

Because resident tax follows last year’s income, not this year’s payslip. A quiet first year followed by a sudden second-year bill is the normal pattern, not an error.

I moved in March. Which city do I pay?

The municipality where your address was registered on January 1 of this year. That January 1 rule fixes the charging city even if you have moved since.

Can I use the deadlines I read online?

Only as a rough rhythm. The four-instalment pattern is common, but exact deadline dates are set by each municipality. The date printed on your slip wins over anything you read online, including this guide.

I changed jobs and received slips for a large amount. Is it a second tax?

Almost certainly not. It is usually the remaining balance of the same annual tax, switched from payroll deduction to ordinary collection because the handover to your new employer has not taken effect.
