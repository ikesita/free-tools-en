Which Suica Should You Get? Welcome Suica vs the App vs a Regular Suica

Japan offers visitors three versions of its tap-to-ride IC card. They look interchangeable — they are not. Choose by trip length and your phone, and you will neither lose a deposit nor strand a balance.

You have just landed in Japan, and before you can ride a single train you have to pick an IC card — the tap-to-ride card that also works as small-change money in shops. Except there isn’t one card. There are three: a red one for tourists, a regular one, and one that lives inside your iPhone. They look interchangeable. They are not. Pick the wrong one and you either lose a deposit you didn’t need to pay, or strand a balance you can never get back.

This guide compares the three options honestly — deposits, expiry, refunds, top-up methods and where each one is actually sold — so the first decision of your trip is the right one.

The short answer

  1. You have an iPhone → get the app version (Welcome Suica Mobile). No lines, no deposit, set it up before you land.
  2. Trip of 28 days or less, no iPhone → the red Welcome Suica card, bought at the airport station.
  3. Long stay, or you want your deposit back → a regular Suica card, sold at station machines again since March 2025.

Option 1: iPhone users — set up before you fly

Download the Welcome Suica Mobile app (iOS, free) and issue your card inside it. You top it up with Apple Pay, and the card is valid for 180 days. One warning: some overseas-issued credit cards are declined for topping up Japanese mobile IC cards — overseas Visa cards are the usual problem, while Mastercard and Amex tend to work. Test a small top-up at home. If your card is refused, plan on a physical card when you land instead.

Option 2: The red Welcome Suica — buy it at the airport station

This is the visitor-only card, sold at dedicated machines at Narita Airport (Terminal 1 and Terminal 2·3 stations) and Haneda Airport Terminal 3 station, and at JR EAST Travel Service Centers (also at Tokyo, Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro, Ueno, Yokohama and Sendai stations). You choose ¥1,000–¥10,000 and the whole amount is spendable — no deposit. The trade-offs: it dies 28 days after the day you buy it, leftover money is never refunded, and sales are limited to one card per person. For a normal holiday, those trade-offs are worth it — as long as you load small and top up often, rather than loading ¥10,000 “to be safe” and donating whatever is left on day 28.

Option 3: The regular Suica — the black machine

At any JR East station, find the black multifunction ticket machine (it says “Commuter Pass / Suica” across the top) or go to a Midori-no-madoguchi ticket office (the staffed “green window”). Sales of these cards were suspended during the global chip shortage, but they resumed in March 2025, so the old advice that “tourists can’t buy a normal Suica anymore” is out of date. The price includes a ¥500 deposit, machines take cash, and the card stays valid for 10 years from last use — keep it as your Japan card for the next trip.

Refunds work like this: at JR East ticket offices in the Suica area, you get the balance minus a ¥220 handling fee, plus your ¥500 deposit back. To buy one, say: Suica o kaitai desu (“I’d like to buy a Suica”).

Topping up — think cash

Top up at ticket machines, at convenience store registers (say “Chāji onegai shimasu” — “top-up, please”), or at Seven Bank ATMs inside 7-Eleven stores. The balance cap is ¥20,000. Welcome Suica cards top up with cash only. The golden rule for the no-refund cards: load small, top up often.

For Welcome Suica and the app, the end-of-trip trick is to spend the last balance at a convenience store — you can pay part by card balance and the rest in cash, down to the last yen.

Trapped at the gate with too little balance?

It happens to everyone once. Your balance is short, the exit gate stays shut and beeps. Do not follow the person in front of you through — that’s fare evasion. Instead, look for the fare adjustment machine (usually inside the gates near the exit) and top up there, or show your card at the staffed gate and pay the difference. Thirty seconds, no drama.

Beyond Tokyo: regional cards

Every region has its own card — ICOCA (Kansai/Osaka), Kitaca (Hokkaido), TOICA (Nagoya area JR), manaca (Nagoya), SUGOCA and Hayakaken (Kyushu) — and they all work in each other’s major cities. Two catches: refunds are only possible in the card’s home region, and some rural one-driver trains and buses still take cash only. Carry coins outside the big cities. And remember: IC cards are one per person — sharing one card between two people breaks the tap-in/tap-out pairing and the gate won’t open.

FAQ

Can I get my Welcome Suica balance back at the airport?

No. Neither the red Welcome Suica nor the app version ever refunds a balance. Spend the last of it at a convenience store, splitting the payment between card balance and cash if needed.

Is the regular Suica really on sale again?

Yes. Sales at black ticket machines and JR ticket offices resumed in March 2025 after the chip-shortage suspension. The ¥500 deposit is included in the purchase price.

My overseas card was declined topping up the app. Now what?

That is a known pattern, especially with overseas Visa cards. Fall back to a physical card — the red Welcome Suica for a short trip, or a regular Suica — and top up with cash.

Can two of us share one card?

No. Each traveler needs their own card; the tap-in and tap-out are paired per person, and a shared card will fail at the gate.
