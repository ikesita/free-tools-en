How to Buy Sumo Tickets Without a Japanese Phone Number

The Japanese ticket site stops you at phone verification. The official English site never asks — here is the route, the calendar, and the sale dates.

You found the tournament dates, chose your day, clicked through the official Japanese ticket site — and hit a wall. Registration demands phone-number verification: a call made from the number on your account. You do not have a Japanese phone number, so the purchase dies right there. Travelers abandon sumo at exactly this point every tournament, assuming the sport is effectively closed to them.

It is not. The Japan Sumo Association’s official English site, Ticket Oosumo English, sells the same tournament tickets with no Japanese phone number and no phone verification at all — name, email, and a credit card are enough. The real obstacles are different ones: sumo runs only six tournaments a year, Tokyo hosts just three of them, and demand is fierce. In 2024, all 90 tournament days sold out. This guide gives you the calendar, the sale dates, and the shortest official route from your sofa to a printed ticket in Japan.

The short answer

  • Buy on Ticket Oosumo English (sumo.pia.jp/en), the English site the Japan Sumo Association itself lists. It is a separate flow from the Japanese site: no membership phone verification, credit card only.
  • Budget a ¥2,000 service fee per ticket on the English site, on top of face value. A four-person box counts as four tickets — ¥8,000 in fees.
  • Tickets are printed at a Seven-Eleven in Japan. Nothing is posted to you and there is no e-ticket on the English site; your seat numbers appear when the ticket prints.
  • Purchases are non-refundable. Check your dates twice before paying, and guard the printed ticket — a lost ticket cannot be reissued for entry.

Step 1: Fix your dates against the six-tournament calendar

Grand Sumo (honbasho) happens six times a year, and nowhere else. Each tournament lasts 15 days, and one ticket covers a full day, from the morning lower-division bouts to the top-division finale around 6 p.m. Most visitors arrive mid-afternoon for the top two divisions.

This calendar decides everything. If your trip misses those windows, there is no grand tournament to buy — plan the trip around the calendar, not the other way round. A Tokyo trip in March, July, or November has no Tokyo sumo; the tournament is in Osaka, Nagoya, or Fukuoka instead. Weekends, the opening day, and the final days sell fastest; midweek days in the middle of the tournament are usually the last to go.

Step 2: Buy on the English site — the shortest route

  1. Go to Ticket Oosumo English and select your tournament and day.
  2. Choose a seat category from those the English site offers for that tournament. The English site sells from its own allocation: a day can show sold out in English while seats remain on the Japanese site, and a few categories — for January 2027, ringside and Box S — are Japanese-site only.
  3. Enter your name and contact details exactly as requested. No Japanese phone number is required in this flow.
  4. Pay by credit card — the only accepted method. Make sure the card is cleared for international online purchases; a bank security check is the most common payment failure.
  5. Save the confirmation email. It carries the instructions for printing your tickets in Japan.

Do not use number-rental services to get past the Japanese site’s phone check. There is no legitimate way around that check, such services breach the platform’s terms, and they can void the ticket. The English site exists precisely so you never need to. Before general sale, some tournaments also run lotteries — if your dates are fixed and popular, entering a lottery when one is offered improves your odds, and the English site announces its own arrangements per tournament, so check its page for your event rather than relying on last year’s pattern.

Step 3: Choose seats — and know the new prices

There are three families of seats. Ringside (tamari) seats are closest to the ring: floor cushions, age 16+ only, no food, drink, or phone use — thrilling and uncomfortable in equal measure, and largely Japanese-site only on the English allocation. Box seats (masu) are floor boxes, usually for four, where you sit on cushions in a sunken square — the classic group experience, priced per person but sold as a box. Tokyo adds a new top grade, Box SS (front two rows), from January 2027. Chair seats are regular stadium chairs on the upper tier — the easiest on backs and budgets, and the mainstay of the English site.

Prices were revised for Tokyo and Osaka from the January 2027 tournament (Nagoya and Fukuoka prices are unchanged). Tokyo per-person prices, tax included, now run from ¥3,000 for a Chair D seat to ¥30,000 for ringside on the final two days, with three price tiers — weekday, weekend/holiday, and days 14–15. As a guide: Chair C is ¥5,000 on a weekday, Chair S is ¥11,000, and Box C is ¥9,500 per person. Always confirm the exact price for your day on the official page before paying — and remember the ¥2,000-per-ticket English-site fee is added on top. On the cheapest seats, that fee is a large share of the total, and on a four-person box it adds ¥8,000.

Step 4: Print at Seven-Eleven, then guard the ticket

After you land in Japan, take your confirmation details to any Seven-Eleven store and print the tickets following the email’s instructions — the store’s multi-copy machine is the usual route. Only then do you see your seat numbers; the English site does not let you pick exact seats. Print early in your trip, not on tournament morning, so any problem can be sorted while offices are open. From that moment, the paper ticket is your only proof of entry. It cannot be replaced if lost, so keep it with your passport, not loose in a pocket.

Step 5: If your day is sold out

Work through the legitimate options only. Try another day of the same tournament — midweek days often remain. Check the official face-value resale service on the Japanese Ticket Oosumo site, where tickets change hands at face value; it is Japanese-language and may require the registration the English site spares you, but it is the Association’s own channel. Or consider a ticket-included tour from a reputable operator — expect to pay well above face value for the convenience, and book early, because these sell out too. What not to do: buy from unofficial resale sites or touts. Reselling tickets above face value for profit is prohibited in Japan under the ticket anti-resale law, the Association warns it takes no responsibility for unauthorized tickets, and a voided ticket at the Kokugikan door is an expensive lesson.

FAQ

Can I really buy without any Japanese phone number?

Yes — on Ticket Oosumo English. Its flow asks for your name, email, and a credit card, with no membership phone verification. The phone-verification wall belongs to the Japanese site; starting there is the most common mistake foreign buyers make.

Will my tickets be emailed or posted to me?

Neither. The English site offers no e-ticket and no postal delivery. You receive a confirmation email, and you print the physical tickets at a Seven-Eleven in Japan. No printout, no entry.

Can I get a refund or change my day?

No. Purchases on the English site are non-refundable, so check the tournament dates and your travel dates twice before paying. If your preferred day sells out, another day of the same tournament is usually the most realistic alternative.

Why does the English site show sold out when the Japanese site still has seats?

The English site sells from its own allocation, and some categories are Japanese-site only. That gap is frustrating but real, and there is no legitimate workaround for the Japanese site’s phone verification — use the English allocation, a lottery when offered, or the official face-value resale service.
