Goshuin for Beginners: How to Start a Shrine Seal Book in Japan, Respectfully

At the shrine office you see silk pouches, paper fortunes, and a clerk writing bold calligraphy into someone’s book. You want the calligraphy. One idea — worship first — explains every rule that follows, and one mistake cannot be undone.

At a shrine office in Japan you will see three counters of beautiful things: small silk pouches, paper fortunes folded into knots, and a clerk writing bold black calligraphy into someone’s book. You want the calligraphy — a goshuin — but you are not sure whether you may ask for one before you have prayed, or whether the tourist stamp you pressed at the station yesterday can go in the same book.

Getting any of this wrong is not dangerous, but it can be quietly embarrassing, and one mistake — mixing sightseeing stamps into the book — cannot be undone. This guide gives you the correct order (worship first, office second), what to say, what to pay, and how to look after the book.

The short answer

  • A goshuin is a record of worship, not a stamp rally. The Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honcho) describes it as something received as proof that you visited and worshipped at the shrine.
  • Worship first, then go to the office. Never ask for a goshuin before you have prayed at the main hall.
  • Goshuin, omamori and omikuji are different things. A goshuin is ink seal and calligraphy in your book. An omamori is a protective amulet in a pouch. An omikuji is a paper fortune slip. Only the goshuin requires worship first and your own book.
  • Use a dedicated book. A goshuincho is used only for goshuin — never for sightseeing stamps, station stamps or autographs.
  • Bring cash. Most offices accept cash only. The offering (hatsuho-ryo) is commonly around ¥300–500 per goshuin; some shrines set a different amount, so check the sign at the window.

Step 1: Worship before anything else

Enter through the torii, rinse your hands at the temizuya water pavilion, then go to the main hall. Offer a coin quietly, bow twice, clap twice, bow once more — the standard Shinto form — and take a moment. Only when you have finished do you look for the office. Regional shrine associations state this plainly: receive the goshuin after worship, and do not make the goshuin itself the purpose of the visit. Even if the office is about to close, worship first — skipping it to beat the clock reverses the meaning of what you are asking for.

Step 2: Find the office, and hand over your book correctly

Look for a building signed 授与所 (juyosho) — the office where amulets and goshuin are awarded. At small shrines it may be a single window, sometimes unattended with a bell to ring. Follow the signs from the main hall rather than heading for the first counter near the gate — that counter often sells amulets only. If you cannot find it, ask: 授与所はどこですか？ (Juyosho wa doko desu ka? — “Where is the shrine office?”).

Open your goshuincho to the next blank page — check which way up it is — and hand it over open, with both hands if you can, saying the one essential phrase: 御朱印をお願いします (Goshuin o onegai shimasu — “A goshuin, please”). Say any special request before they start writing. Then step back and wait quietly: do not lean over the counter or photograph the writing without permission. Have coins and small notes ready, and follow the clerk’s gesture for whether the offering is taken before or after writing.

Step 3: Written in your book, or written on a sheet?

There are two forms, and both are legitimate. With direct writing (chokugaki), the clerk writes in your book on the spot. With a pre-written sheet (kakioki), you receive a finished sheet to paste into your book at home. Busy shrines, festival days and small shrines with one priest use kakioki often. The window sign usually says 書置き (kakioki, “pre-written”) when only sheets are available — you can also ask 書置きですか？ (Kakioki desu ka?). Accept it without disappointment: the worship it records is identical. Paste it in promptly with a glue stick, in visit order.

Step 4: Omikuji and omamori, briefly and correctly

An omikuji fortune is drawn after worship too, typically for ¥100–200. There is no single national ranking of fortunes: the grade names — daikichi (great blessing) down through kichi, chukichi, shokichi and suekichi to kyo (curse) — and their order vary by shrine. Read your shrine’s list on the box or board. If the fortune is bad, the custom is to tie it to the shrine’s designated rack or branches (omikuji-kake) and leave it behind — only in the designated place, never on random trees or fences. Keeping a good fortune is fine.

An omamori amulet needs no book and no procedure: choose it at the juyosho and receive it with both hands. Treat it as a sacred object, not a keyring souvenir — keep it in your bag or at home, not on the floor.

Step 5: Look after the book

Keep the goshuincho in a clean, dry place — ideally in its protective cover or a cloth pouch, stored somewhere high and tidy at home. Write your name inside the cover so a lost book can find its way back. Use it only for shrine and temple goshuin: temples award goshuin too, in the same kind of book, and mixing shrine and temple pages in one book is widely accepted. What never belongs in it: tourist stamps, station stamps, museum stamps, signatures, or your own notes. Once a sightseeing stamp is pressed on a page, no office can remove it — and some offices will decline to write in a book used that way. Never present it as a passport or ID, either: it is a religious record, not a document.

Two more useful phrases: 御朱印帳はありますか？ (Goshuincho wa arimasu ka? — “Do you sell goshuin books?”). Close with ありがとうございました (Arigato gozaimashita — “Thank you very much”).

FAQ

How much does a goshuin cost?

The offering, called hatsuho-ryo, is commonly around ¥300–500 per goshuin, paid in cash. Some shrines set a different amount, so check the sign at the office window.

I was handed a loose sheet instead of having my book written in. Is that lesser?

No. A pre-written kakioki sheet is normal practice at busy and small shrines alike, and it records the same worship. Paste it into your book in visit order with a glue stick.

Can I put station stamps in the same book to save space?

Never. Sightseeing and station stamps belong in a separate travel notebook. A mixed book cannot be fixed, and some offices will refuse to write in one.

Is a bad omikuji fortune the same grade everywhere?

No. Fortune grade names and their order differ by shrine — there is no national ranking. Read the list shown at the shrine where you drew it.
