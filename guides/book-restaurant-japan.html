How to Book Popular Restaurants in Japan in English: TableCheck, Tabelog, OMAKASE — and the Phone-Only Wall

The eight-seat sushi counter everyone recommends takes bookings by phone, in Japanese, from regulars. You are not being singled out — but there is a ladder of routes to try, in order, and each rung charges a different fee.

You found it: the eight-seat sushi counter everyone recommends, the kaiseki place worth building a Kyoto day around. Then the booking wall goes up. The restaurant’s page is Japanese only. Reservations are “by phone.” The online form rejects your number because it is not a Japanese mobile. This is how many of Japan’s best small restaurants have always taken bookings — and the regulars they serve already know the system.

This guide compares the English booking platforms honestly, including what each one charges, then covers the two routes that work when no platform lists your restaurant: calling with a Japanese script, and asking your hotel concierge properly. It also tells you, plainly, when no route exists at all.

The short answer

Work down this ladder, in order. Stop at the first rung that lists your restaurant.

  1. TableCheck — the restaurant’s own booking engine, in English. No platform fee for a standard booking, though the restaurant may set a card hold or prepayment.
  2. Tabelog (English/multilingual site) — ¥440 per person, charged on confirmation.
  3. OMAKASE — specialist for top sushi and fine-dining counters. ¥390 per seat, charged on booking.
  4. byFood — English, concierge-style booking. About ¥2,000 per person for seat-only reservations.
  5. Your hotel concierge — ask them to call. Usually free for staying guests.
  6. Phone it yourself — with a prepared Japanese script.

Two warnings apply to every rung: platform fees are generally not refunded if you cancel, and same-day cancellation is commonly charged at 100% of the course price.

Step 1: Start from the restaurant’s own booking route

Start from the restaurant itself, not a search engine’s summary. Its website, social media or Tabelog page usually names the service that takes its bookings — a TableCheck link, an OMAKASE page, a phone number, or nothing at all. That named route is the legitimate one. If the page says 電話のみ (denwa nomi, “phone only”), believe it: no platform has a secret back door.

Step 2: What each platform is actually good at

TableCheck runs the booking pages of a huge range of restaurants, from hotel dining to Michelin counters. The flow works in English, accepts overseas phone numbers, and charges no platform fee for a standard reservation. What varies is the restaurant’s own setting: some take a card hold, some require prepayment, and a paid FastPass option (from around ¥390) exists at a few queue-heavy shops. Read the cancellation text on the confirmation screen — that is the contract.

Tabelog is Japan’s dominant review site; its English booking service covers a smaller, bookable subset of its listings. You register a card, choose a set course, and pay a ¥440 per person system fee on confirmation, generally kept if you cancel. The meal is paid at the restaurant.

OMAKASE is the specialist door to top-tier sushi and kaiseki counters, many releasing seats in timed rounds that sell out in minutes. It runs in English, requires phone verification and a registered credit card — debit and prepaid cards are not accepted — and charges ¥390 per seat when the booking completes, non-refundable. Register and verify in advance, not on release day.

byFood is the concierge model: English listings, a human-backed request process, and, for seat-only reservations, a concierge fee of ¥2,000 per person according to its own FAQ. That fee does not go toward your meal, and it is refunded if the restaurant cancels. It costs the most of the four — and for a phone-only restaurant it may be the only online route.

Step 3: Read the strict terms before you confirm — they are real

Small counters buy ingredients for your seat that morning, so their terms are not boilerplate. A typical cancellation ladder charges a percentage from several days out, rising to 100% on the day, charged to your registered card without a reminder call. Many counters treat arrival 15 minutes late as a cancellation. On OMAKASE, dates and times generally cannot be changed: you cancel, lose the fee, and book again. Perfume, photo and phone rules are listed in the confirmation email — read it before the day, not in the taxi.

One practical consequence: do not book the trip’s highlight meal for your first night. A delayed flight plus a same-day 100% charge is the most expensive version of jet lag. Put the special meal mid-trip.

Step 4: Phone-only? Call with a script — or delegate properly

If you call yourself, call during the restaurant’s stated hours — never during lunch or dinner service — and speak slowly; the numbers matter most. The essential line is 予約をお願いしたいのですが (Yoyaku o onegai shitai no desu ga — “I’d like to make a reservation”), followed by the date, time and party size, your name and phone number (give your hotel’s number if your mobile will not work in Japan), and your allergies — or アレルギーはありません (Arerugī wa arimasen, “I have no allergies”). Repeat the date and time back before you hang up. At the door, show: 予約した [your name] です (Yoyaku shita [name] desu — “I’m [name], I have a reservation”).

The stronger tool is often your hotel concierge: restaurants take their calls seriously. Send the request in writing, as soon as you have booked the hotel, because popular counters fill a month or more out. Include the restaurant name, preferred date plus alternatives, time, party size, course or budget, and allergies — and state that you accept a cancellation policy that may charge up to 100% on the day.

One route to avoid: automated proxy-calling services that place robocalls on your behalf. Restaurants have publicly complained about machine-made and duplicate bookings, and some now refuse them. Use a platform the restaurant chose, a human concierge, or your own voice.

Step 5: When the answer is “regulars only” — believe it, and pivot

Some celebrated rooms are ikkensan o-kotowari (一見さんお断り): they do not accept first-time guests at all, and bookings can only be made through an existing regular. No fee, script or concierge can override it. Pivot instead: the same chef’s second restaurant, a counter on OMAKASE or TableCheck in the same style, or lunch, which at many kaiseki and sushi restaurants is both easier to book and cheaper. One special meal you actually eat beats three famous doors that never open.

FAQ

Do the platform fees come off my bill at the restaurant?

No. The Tabelog ¥440, OMAKASE ¥390 and byFood fees are service charges. They do not reduce what you pay for the meal, and cancelling does not bring them back.

Do I need a Japanese phone number to book?

Not always. TableCheck and OMAKASE accept overseas numbers. What blocks people is leaving registration until release day — set accounts up in advance.

How late is too late?

Many counters treat 15 minutes late as a cancellation, commonly charged at 100% of the course price. Arrive early, not on time.

A service says it can call any restaurant for me automatically. Should I use it?

No. Restaurants have complained publicly about automated proxy calls and duplicate bookings, and some refuse them.
