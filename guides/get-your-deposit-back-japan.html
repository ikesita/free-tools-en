Getting Your Apartment Deposit Back in Japan: The Genjo Kaifuku Rules Explained

You cleaned, handed back the keys, and got a settlement sheet that eats the whole deposit. Restoration in Japan does not mean returning the home to like-new condition — here is the official yardstick the bill is measured against.

You cleaned the apartment, handed back the keys, and waited for your deposit (shikikin, 敷金). Instead you receive a settlement sheet (seisan-sho, 精算書): full-room wallpaper replacement, re-covering of every tatami, a cleaning fee — a total that leaves almost nothing. Nothing explains why each item is yours to pay for. Totals do not move, but individual lines do, once checked against the right standard.

That standard is public. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) publishes the guideline on restoration disputes that landlords, agents, and consumer centers measure these bills against, and the Civil Code backs its central principle. This guide explains what is yours and what is not, and the exact questions — in Japanese — to ask in writing before you agree to anything. It is general information, not legal advice.

The short answer

  • Restoration (genjo kaifuku, 原状回復) does not mean like-new. Under the MLIT guideline and Civil Code Article 621, you pay only for damage caused by your intent, negligence, breach of your duty of care, or use beyond normal living.
  • Normal wear and aging are the landlord’s cost. Normal wear (通常損耗) and aging (経年変化) are, in principle, already covered by your rent.
  • Your share shrinks with time. Even for damage that is yours, elapsed years reduce what you owe — for wallpaper, value is treated as falling over an assumed life of commonly six years, down to a residual value of 1 yen.
  • You pay for the damaged part, not the whole room. Charges are calculated in small units: wallpaper per square meter, tatami per mat, fusuma per panel.
  • Two limits matter. The guideline is a widely used standard, not a law with binding force, and a special clause (特約) in your lease can shift some costs — so read your contract alongside it.

Who pays for what: the guideline line

The same mark can fall on either side depending on cause and degree. Sun fading, furniture dents, and dark marks behind a TV or fridge are usually the landlord’s. Tobacco stains and smell, drink stains left uncleaned, and pet scratches are usually the tenant’s. Tatami discolored by sunlight is the landlord’s; spills, burns, and mold from condensation you left alone are yours. Pin holes needing no board repair are normally the landlord’s; large screw holes, graffiti, and damage from moving furniture are yours. A lock change when no key was lost is normally the landlord’s; a lost key, and cleaning needed because normal cleaning was not done, are yours.

Tokyo adds its own layer, the Tokyo Rules. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government’s Ordinance for the Prevention of Residential Rental Disputes requires the broker, before you sign, to explain in a separate document how restoration costs are split and any special clauses. Keep that document and compare your bill against it. And remember: shikikin, the deposit, is refundable after valid deductions, while reikin (礼金, key money) is not refundable by design.

Why the years you lived there change the bill

Two principles do most of the work. The first is elapsed-years reduction. For wallpaper and carpet, the guideline treats value as falling over an assumed useful life — commonly six years for wallpaper — down to a residual value of 1 yen. If wallpaper was new when you moved in and you damaged it after three years, your share is roughly half the replacement cost; after six years it is close to zero. Ask which useful life and move-in value were used for every line: a sheet charging 100 percent after a long tenancy has skipped this step.

The second principle is unit-based charging. Charges are calculated in small units — wallpaper per square meter, with one side of a room as a practical minimum in some cases; tatami per mat; fusuma per panel. One stained section does not, by itself, justify re-papering an entire apartment at your cost. If pattern matching forces a wider job, that reasoning should be explained on the sheet.

Check the sheet line by line, and ask in writing

Start with evidence. Photos with dates are the strongest thing you can bring: shoot in daylight at move-in and again at move-out after cleaning, wide shot first, then a close-up of every flaw with a coin or ruler for scale. Note anything already damaged at move-in on the agent’s condition sheet and keep a copy. Do not sign the settlement on inspection day; you can say you will review it at home.

Check each line against three questions: is this normal wear, or damage I caused? Was the elapsed-years reduction applied? And what unit and area were charged? Red flags are wear items with no cause given, full price after several years, whole-room lump sums, and fees nowhere in your contract. Put your questions in writing: Uchiwake o oshiete kudasai — “Please show me the breakdown.” Keika nensu wa dono yō ni keisan shite imasu ka? — “How did you calculate the elapsed years?” Tsūjō sonmo de wa nai riyū o oshiete kudasai — “Please explain why this is not normal wear.” Mitsumorisho o misete kudasai — “Please show me the estimate.” Keiyakusho no dono tokuyaku ni motozukimasu ka? — “Which special clause in the lease is this based on?” And: Shomen de kaitō o onegai shimasu — “Please reply in writing.”

If you cannot agree, Consumer Hotline 188 (消費者ホットライン), under the Consumer Affairs Agency, connects you to your nearest consumer affairs center; consultation is free, the call is charged, and service is mainly in Japanese. Houterasu, the Japan Legal Support Center, has a multilingual information line, 0570-078377 on weekdays. Bring your lease, photo log, and settlement sheet.

FAQ

Does the MLIT guideline automatically win my dispute?

No. It is a widely used standard, not a statute. Your lease’s special clauses and the actual facts still matter — which is exactly why the public consultation desks exist.

I lived there six years and was charged full price for wallpaper. Is that right?

It deserves a question. Under the guideline’s approach, wallpaper value falls over an assumed life of commonly six years to a residual 1 yen, so a full-price charge after six years has likely skipped the elapsed-years reduction. Ask how it was calculated.

Can the landlord re-paper the whole room for one stain?

In principle, charges are limited to the damaged part, calculated per square meter with one wall as a practical minimum in some cases. A wider job needs an explanation, such as pattern matching — ask for that reasoning on the sheet.

Is key money part of what I get back?

No. The deposit (shikikin) is refundable after valid deductions. Key money (reikin) is non-refundable by design, so focus your check on the deposit settlement.
