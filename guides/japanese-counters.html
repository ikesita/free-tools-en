Japanese Counters (Josushi): The 12 That Actually Happen, With the Sound Changes

You can count to ten in Japanese — then you try to order two bottled beers and freeze, because the number changes shape depending on what you are counting. Twelve counters cover almost every real transaction, and their sound changes follow one pattern.

You walk into an izakaya, you know your numbers, you try to order two bottled beers — and you stall. Is it ni-hon? Something else? The staff member waits, you hold up two fingers, and the order gets through on gesture alone. Nothing about your Japanese is broken: Japanese numbers rarely travel alone. Almost everything you count needs a counter word — a josūshi (助数詞) — chosen by the kind of thing being counted, and a handful of counters change the sound of the number itself. Textbooks list hundreds of counters; real life in shops, restaurants, ticket offices and hotels uses about twelve. Learn those twelve, with their sounds, and the freezing stops.

The short answer

  • Every kind of thing has its counter. Bottles take 本 (hon), tickets 枚 (mai), drinks in a glass 杯 (hai), people 人 (nin). No single phrase for “two of those” fits everything.
  • Only a few counters change sounds, in the same places: at 1, 6, 8 and 10 the counter tightens, and at 3, counters beginning with h soften. Learn that one pattern instead of twelve lists.
  • Two counters are irregular at the start. One person is ひとり (hitori), two are ふたり (futari) — not ichi-nin, ni-nin. Days use a native series: the 2nd is ふつか (futsuka), the 3rd みっか (mikka).
  • You always have an escape route. If the counter will not come, the 〜つ series (ひとつ, ふたつ…) or 〜個 (ko) plus a gesture and 〜をください (o kudasai, “please”) will be understood.

Two warning labels. 本 (hon) is not only for stick-shaped objects: a phone call, a train service and a film can also be 一本 (ippon). And 杯 (hai) counts the container, not the liquid — the same beer is 一本 (ippon) in a bottle, 一杯 (ippai) in a glass.

The pattern: 1, 6, 8 and 10 squeeze (いち + ほん → いっぽん); 3 softens the h-counters (さん + ほん → さんぼん; さん + はい → さんばい). 枚 (mai) and 台 (dai) never change: いちまい, さんまい, いちだい, さんだい. 泊 (haku) follows the h pattern — いっぱく, さんぱく — and 足 (soku) the s pattern: いっそく, はっそく.

The two irregulars: people and days

People (〜人). ひとり (hitori) for one, ふたり (futari) for two. From three it settles: さんにん (sannin), よにん (yonin — never shinin), ごにん, ろくにん, しちにん／ななにん, はちにん, きゅうにん, じゅうにん. “How many people?” is なんにん (nannin). Staff may ask with the polite counter, なんめいさま (nanmei-sama) — you still answer ふたりです or さんにんです.

Days (〜日). Dates and durations use the native series for 1–10: ついたち (tsuitachi, the 1st only), ふつか (futsuka), みっか (mikka), よっか (yokka), いつか (itsuka), むいか (muika), なのか (nanoka), ようか (yōka), ここのか (kokonoka), とおか (tōka). Watch 14日 (jūyokka), 20日 (hatsuka) and 24日 (nijūyokka). The same forms give durations: ふつか is both “the 2nd” and “two days.”

Listening: staff answer with the real counter

Even if you order with the safe 〜つ series, staff usually confirm with the proper counter. Order ふたつ (futatsu) and you will hear ビール、にほんですね for two bottled beers; みっつ (mittsu) comes back as チケット、さんまいですね for three tickets; ひとつ (hitotsu) as コーヒー、いっぱいですね for one coffee; and a table for two as ふたりですね.

The 〜つ series runs ひとつ, ふたつ, みっつ, よっつ, いつつ, むっつ, ななつ, やっつ, ここのつ, とお (tō, ten — no つ), then stops. Above ten, switch to 〜個: じゅういっこ (jūikko), じゅうにこ (jūniko).

Say these out loud: five scenes

  • Izakaya: ビールをにほんください。 — “Two bottled beers, please.” As a draft: なまビールをにはいください.
  • Ticket office: チケットをさんまいください。 — “Three tickets, please.”
  • Restaurant booking: よにんでよやくしたいです。 — “I’d like to book for four people.” For two: ふたりです.
  • Hotel: さんぱくよていです。 — “I’m staying three nights.” さんぱく, not sanhaku.
  • Shop: これをふたつと、それをいっこください。 — “Two of these, one of those.”

If the counter won’t come, use the escape hatch: point, hold up your fingers, and say これをふたつください (Kore o futatsu kudasai). It isn’t elegant, but it is correct Japanese and nobody will misunderstand you. One error is worth unlearning early: いちほん and さんほん for bottles — the most-heard learner mistake — are always いっぽん (ippon) and さんぼん (sanbon). Counters for rabbits, tatami mats and shrine gates exist too; treat them as trivia and let the twelve above do the work.

FAQ

What if I forget the counter completely?

Use 〜つ (up to ten) or 〜個, add a finger gesture, and finish with 〜をください. Staff will understand, and usually repeat it back with the correct counter.

Why is one beer sometimes ippon and sometimes ippai?

Because 杯 (hai) counts the container, not the drink: bottled, 一本 (ippon); in a glass, 一杯 (ippai).

How do I say a table for two?

ふたりです (Futari desu). One and two people are irregular — ひとり and ふたり — and 〜にん only behaves regularly from three up.

How many counters do I actually need?

About twelve — these cover ordering, tickets, bookings, shopping and stays.
