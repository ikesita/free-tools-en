Your Baby's First 30 Days in Japan: The 14-Day, 15-Day and 30-Day Deadlines

One birth starts at least five clocks at once, at four different counters, and they are not even counted the same way. This guide puts them all on a single timeline counted from Day 0: the day of birth.

Your baby was born in Japan yesterday, and somewhere in the discharge paperwork is a form you have never seen before. Nobody explains that the birth starts at least five clocks at once: a birth registration counted including the birthday, a child allowance counted from the day after the birth, an immigration application due within 30 days, a health insurance enrollment nobody files for you, and a ¥500,000 childbirth payment that mostly moves between your insurer and the hospital.

Each filing lives at a different counter — city hall, your employer, the regional immigration bureau, your embassy. Miss the wrong one and the cost is real: child allowance months missed through a late application are, in principle, never paid, and an unregistered baby has no residence record, which blocks almost everything else. The whole sequence fits on one timeline, and most of it can be done in two or three trips if you aim at target days rather than legal last days. If one parent is Japanese, the family register (koseki) steps differ in places.

The money, in brief: the lump-sum childbirth allowance (shussan ikuji ichijikin) is in principle ¥500,000 per child, paid by your health insurer — ¥488,000 if the facility is not enrolled in the obstetrical compensation system. The child allowance is ¥15,000 a month under age 3, ¥10,000 from age 3 to the end of high school age, and ¥30,000 for a third and later child.

At the hospital, and the birth registration (Day 0–13)

The hospital issues the birth certificate (shussei shōmeisho), usually as one half of the combined birth registration form. Guard it: everything else builds on it. Before discharge, confirm the hospital uses the direct payment system (chokusetsu shiharai seido) — most do. Your insurer then pays the allowance straight to the hospital; you pay only any amount above ¥500,000. The agreement is signed before or at delivery. If delivery costs less, the difference is yours to claim, so keep the itemized statement (naimeisho) and your Mother and Child Health Handbook (boshi kenkō techō).

File the birth registration (shussei todoke) at the municipal office of the place of birth or where you live — your home ward office is the practical choice. The window is within 14 days counting the day of birth itself, so the last day is Day 13; an office holiday moves it to the next open day. No fee. Bring the form with the birth certificate attached, the Mother and Child Health Handbook, and the filing parent’s ID and residence card. Once accepted, ask for a certificate of acceptance (shussei todoke juri shōmeisho) — immigration will ask for it — and a residence record copy (jūminhyō) showing the whole household.

The child allowance: a separate application, a different count (by Day 15)

The child allowance (jidō teate) is not automatic and does not come with the birth registration: it is a separate application at a separate desk, and the step tired parents most often lose money on. In principle it is paid from the month after you apply. The protection is the 15-day rule described by the Children and Families Agency: apply within 15 days counted from the day after the birth — Day 15 — and your start month is protected even if you apply in the next calendar month. Apply later and the missed months are, in principle, gone.

For a first child, file the certification request (nintei seikyūsho); for a second or later child, the amount-revision version — ask the desk which applies. Public servants apply through their employer. Bring the applicant parent’s bank details. One trap: the allowance is always filed where you live, not near the hospital. At city hall, one sentence covers the main trip: Shussei todoke to jidō teate no shinsei o shitai desu. Awasete, shussei todoke juri shōmeisho to, setai zen’in ga kisai sareta jūminhyō no utsushi o kudasai — “I’d like to file the birth registration and apply for the child allowance, and get the certificate of acceptance and a whole-household residence record copy.”

Health insurance, then immigration (Day 7–30)

Your baby joins the same system you are in. On employee health insurance, enrollment runs through your employer: tell HR and ask for the dependent-enrollment form, because nobody notifies them for you. On National Health Insurance, enroll at city hall, often on the same visit. Keep every clinic receipt until coverage is confirmed. Also ask about your municipality’s infant medical expense subsidy (kodomo iryōhi josei): age limits and conditions differ by municipality, so ask at your own counter.

Finally, immigration. A baby born in Japan to two foreign parents does not acquire Japanese nationality by birth alone, and has no status of residence automatically. If the baby will remain in Japan more than 60 days, file an application for acquisition of a status of residence (zairyū shikaku shutoku kyoka shinsei) at the regional immigration bureau within 30 days of the birth; the usual outcome is a status such as Dependent. Bring the application form from the Immigration Services Agency, the certificate of acceptance, a whole-household residence record copy, and both parents’ passports and residence cards. Filing around Day 14–21 leaves a buffer if a document needs re-issuing. Separately, register the birth with your own country’s embassy and apply for the baby’s passport there.

FAQ

Does the birth registration cover the other filings too?

No. It creates the record. It does not apply for the child allowance, enroll the baby in health insurance, or give the baby a status of residence — each of those is its own application.

Why do the deadlines count differently?

Each system defines its own period: 14 days including the birthday for the registration, 15 days from the day after for the allowance, and 30 days from the birth for immigration.

How much is the childbirth allowance, and who pays it?

In principle ¥500,000 per child, paid by your health insurer — ¥488,000 if the facility is not enrolled in the obstetrical compensation system. Under direct payment it usually goes straight to the hospital.
