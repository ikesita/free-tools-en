Lost Something in Japan? How the Lost & Found System Works

The train doors close — and your bag is still on it. Japan's system gets things back at a rate that surprises most visitors, but only if you contact the right desk, quickly, with the right details.

Japan returns lost property at a rate that surprises most visitors — wallets come back with the cash inside, phones resurface days later. But there is no single national lost-and-found number. A bag left on a train, a phone left in a taxi, and a wallet dropped on the street each go to a different place and follow a different clock. This guide covers who to contact, what to tell them, and how long you have.

The short answer

  1. Go back to the operator first. Lost it on a train? Contact that railway company. In a taxi? That taxi company. In a shop or hotel? That business. On the street, or not sure? The nearest kōban (police box).
  2. Do it today. Operators typically hold items for only a few days before handing them to the police. Speed matters more than perfect Japanese.
  3. Details find things. Line, time, carriage, seat, brand, colour, and one thing inside the item are what staff match against their shelves.
  4. The police are the final destination. Items passed to the police are kept for three months under the Lost Property Act, and found items in Tokyo are searchable online during that period. Treat three months as a hard deadline.

The train rule: "JR" is not one company

The most common beginner mistake is searching for "JR lost and found" as if it were a single desk. JR is a group of separate regional companies — JR East, JR Central, JR West, JR Hokkaido, JR Shikoku, and JR Kyushu — and each runs its own lost-property system, alongside every private railway and subway operator. A phone left on a JR East train near Tokyo and a bag left on a JR West train near Osaka live in different databases. Always name the company and the line, not just "the train." JR East, for example, takes enquiries through its lost-property chat and a dedicated phone line (050-2016-1601, 8:00–20:00), and found items are registered into its system before being passed to a police station once the holding period ends. Other companies offer similar routes — station staff can point you to the right one in seconds.

What to tell them: the information template

Staff match items against shelves using exactly these fields. Have them ready before you call or open the chat form:

  • When: date and approximate time you lost it
  • Where: line and direction / shop name / taxi route (from → to)
  • Position: carriage number and seat, if on a train
  • The item: type, brand, colour, size, distinctive marks or stickers
  • Inside it: one or two contents only you would know — offices often ask this to verify the item is yours
  • Your contact: a phone number that works in Japan (a hotel number is fine) and an email address

"A black bag" matches fifty shelves. "A black bag, carriage 7, with a red notebook and a Suica card inside" matches one.

Taxis: the receipt is the key

A Japanese taxi receipt prints the company name, the vehicle number, and the time — with those three, the company can identify the exact car and driver, and recovery is usually fast. Booked through an app such as GO? Your trip history serves as the receipt. No receipt and no app record? Give the dispatcher the pickup point, destination, and time, and file a police report as well. One trap: industry bodies such as the Tokyo Taxi Center do not handle lost property — always go to the operating company itself.

If it was your passport: do these in order

The order is the point:

  1. Check the operator first (railway, taxi, hotel). Passports are handed in constantly and may simply be waiting at a desk.
  2. File a lost-property report at a kōban or police station — ishitsu todoke (遺失届) if you lost it, a theft report if you believe it was stolen. Keep the report receipt and its number safe, and photograph them.
  3. Contact your embassy or consulate with that police paperwork. They will explain their requirements — typically the police report, passport photos, proof of identity or citizenship such as a copy of the lost passport, and a fee — and whether they issue an emergency travel document or a full replacement.
  4. Ask about your entry record. Your landing-permission sticker was in the lost passport; your embassy or the immigration authorities will tell you whether you need an endorsement transfer or departure paperwork before you fly.

FAQ

How long do I have to recover a lost item?

Operators typically hold items for only a short period — often a few days — before handing them to the police, who keep items for three months under the Lost Property Act. Contact the operator the same day.

I have already left Japan. Can my item be posted to me overseas?

Usually not. Many operators and police counters handle collection in person only. Ask early about authorising someone in Japan to collect it for you.

Should I file the police report before or after going to my embassy for a lost passport?

Before. Embassies generally require the police paperwork first, so file the ishitsu todoke, keep the report number, and then go to the embassy.

Can a central taxi or JR service search for me?

No. There is no single JR desk and no central taxi desk. The company that operated your train or your taxi is the only first call that works.
