Moving to Japan — The Paperwork Order That Actually Works in Your First 90 Days

The phone shop wants a bank account. The bank wants a registered address. Nobody hands you the order — so here it is.

You have just landed in Japan with a residence card, two suitcases, and a list of things everyone tells you to do at once: get a phone, open a bank account, sort out health insurance, get your My Number. So you walk into a phone shop — and are told you need a Japanese bank account or credit card first. You walk into a bank — and are told your address must be registered and printed on the back of your residence card first. You have not registered anything yet, because you were busy trying to buy a phone.

None of those counters is wrong. Each is describing its own requirement accurately. Almost every frustration new residents describe comes down to one thing: Japanese procedures form a dependency chain, and the problem is purely the order. This guide gives you that order — address registration, then My Number, insurance and pension, then bank, then phone — and explains why each step unlocks the next.

The dependency chain, on one page

Everything in your first 90 days hangs off one document and one visit. The document is your residence card (zairyu card), issued at the airport if you land at one of the major airports — New Chitose, Sendai, Narita, Haneda, Chubu Centrair, Kansai, Kobe, Hiroshima, Fukuoka, or Naha — and mailed to you later if you land elsewhere. The visit is your first trip to the municipal office (city hall or ward office) of the place you will live. Read the chain as: you cannot do B until A is done.

  1. Address registration first. Your address is printed on the back of your residence card. Until that happens, your card is in its “before” state, and nearly every counter in Japan will look at the back first.
  2. My Number is assigned at registration. You do not apply for the number; it is created for you when you register, and a paper Individual Number Notice is mailed to your new address afterward, typically within a few weeks.
  3. Health insurance and pension enrollment happen at the same office, ideally in the same visit, if an employer is not covering you.
  4. A certificate of residence (juminhyo) — the paper you buy at that same counter — unlocks much of what follows. Get three copies while you stand there; typical fee is ¥200–400 per copy, set by each municipality.
  5. Bank account next, then phone contract. Banks want the residence card with the address printed; carriers want the card plus a payment method — a Japanese bank account or a credit card.

The four deadlines that actually matter

Almost everything else — bank, phone, the plastic My Number Card, a driver’s license — has no hard legal deadline. It is sequenced by dependency, not by law. These four are different:

14 daysAddress registration (moving-in notice, tennyu todoke)The day you settle at your address
14 daysNational Health Insurance enrollment (if not covered by an employer)The day you become eligible — usually registration day
14 daysNational Pension enrollment (if not covered by an employer)The day you become eligible
14 daysNotification to Immigration of your employer (joining or leaving)The day of the change

Note the first clock carefully: the 14 days run from when you settle — when you actually start living at the address — not from your landing date. If you land on the 1st, stay in a hotel for a week, and move into your apartment on the 8th, your clock starts on the 8th. Late filing is accepted, but it can complicate health insurance backdating, so file on time; there is no advantage in waiting.

Step 1 (Days 1–14): One municipal office visit, four procedures

Treat this visit as four procedures, not one. People who treat it as “just registering an address” end up coming back three more times. Bring your residence card (or passport, if your card is being mailed later), your passport, your exact address including building name and room number, and cash for certificates.

Counter 1 — Resident registration

File the moving-in notice. The clerk prints your address on the back of your residence card, and from that moment the rest of this guide unlocks. At the same counter, buy your certificates of residence. Ask for the version with your My Number printed on it only if a specific procedure asks for it — a certificate showing the number is a sensitive document, and the default version, which omits it, is what most counters want.

Counters 2 and 3 — Health insurance and pension

Japan’s rule is simple: every resident is covered, always, by exactly one health insurance system and one pension system. There is no opt-out and no gap allowed. If your employer enrolls you in employee social insurance, you skip these counters. If not, you enroll yourself — in National Health Insurance (NHI) and the National Pension — at this same office, in this same visit.

For NHI, premiums are charged retroactively to the month you became eligible whether you filed or not, but care received before filing may have to be paid in full out of pocket. File in the same visit. Since 2 December 2024, paper health insurance cards are no longer issued; you receive a Certificate of Eligibility to show at clinics instead, or use a My Number Card registered for health insurance use once you have one. At the clinic counter you generally pay 30% of the medical cost — the standard working-age rate — and NHI covers the rest. Premiums are set by your municipality, mainly from your previous year’s income in Japan, so a new arrival with no previous-year Japan income is assessed at the low end in the first year. The bill often surprises people in year two, when it is recalculated on a full year of Japanese salary.

For the National Pension, every resident aged 20 to 59 must be enrolled. If an employer enrolls you in the Employees’ Pension, that automatically includes the National Pension and you do nothing. Everyone else pays a flat national amount: ¥17,920 per month for FY2026 (April 2026 – March 2027); it was ¥17,510 in FY2025 and is revised every April. If you cannot pay, do not simply stop and stay silent — formal full or partial exemption and deferral systems exist, including a student special payment system, and approved months protect your record in ways unpaid months do not. In your first year, with no previous-year Japan income, many new residents qualify for an exemption — ask at the counter when you enroll.

Counter 4 — My Number

Your number is assigned as a result of Counter 1. Guard the paper notice when it arrives by mail: it is your only paper proof of the number until the plastic card arrives, and banks and employers will ask to see one or the other. The plastic My Number Card itself is a separate application — after the notice arrives, apply online, by post, or at the municipal office. First issuance is free, processing takes roughly one month in most municipalities, and you collect the card in person at the office and set PINs at the counter. It is technically optional, but in practice get it: it is the most widely accepted photo ID you will own in Japan, it can work at clinics in place of the paper certificate, and it lets you print certificates at convenience stores.

Step 2 (Weeks 2–4): Bank first, then phone

This is where the dependency map earns its keep. At the bank, the standard set is: residence card with your address printed on the back, passport, your My Number notice or card if it has arrived, a phone number where the bank can reach you, and cash for a small initial deposit. A personal seal (hanko) is not required for foreign residents at most major banks — a signature is accepted.

Japan Post Bank (Yucho) is the traditional first account for new arrivals, with branches inside post offices everywhere and the most predictable requirements for recently arrived residents. Major city banks and online banks vary more: residents in Japan for less than six months are treated as “non-residents” by many banks, and some account functions — notably international remittances — may be restricted during that period. That is bank policy, not a single national rule. If the first bank says no, try Japan Post Bank rather than concluding accounts are impossible. Your cash card is usually mailed to your registered address within one to two weeks. Give your employer the account details as soon as they exist — and give them exactly as printed, because the katakana rendering of your name on the account must match payroll transfers. A name mismatch is the most common cause of a failed first salary payment.

Only then, the phone. Under Japan’s mobile phone identity-verification law, carriers must verify your identity and address — in practice, your residence card with the address printed. For a standard postpaid contract, major carriers also require a Japanese bank account for direct debit or a credit card. That is exactly the deadlock, and why the bank comes first. While the chain resolves, bridge options keep you online: a travel data eSIM, a data-only prepaid SIM, or a rental pocket Wi-Fi. But most of those do not give you a Japanese voice number (070/080/090), and that number is quietly required by bank SMS verification, delivery services, municipal online services, and two-factor authentication for Japanese services. Treat data-only as a bridge, and plan to hold a real contract by the end of your first month. One more practical tip: carrier handset installment plans involve a credit check that new residents with no Japanese credit history often fail. Buying a SIM-free handset outright and pairing it with a SIM-only contract removes that failure point entirely.

Step 3 (Months 2–3): The filing your employer does not do for you

If an employer is not covering you, then on registration day you personally enroll in both NHI and the National Pension, at the same office, in the same visit. That sentence, followed, prevents the two most expensive mistakes new residents make.

If you are employed, your employer files your health insurance, Employees’ Pension, and employment insurance — but one filing is yours alone: the notification of the accepting organization to the Immigration Services Agency. When you start working for an employer (or change or leave one), you personally must notify Immigration within 14 days of the change, online, by post, or at a regional immigration office. New hires routinely miss this because they assume HR handles all paperwork. HR does not handle this one. Also calendar your period-of-stay expiry date now: renewal applications generally open about three months before expiry, and a record of on-time address, insurance, and pension filings is exactly what makes that renewal easy.

Two classic deadlocks, resolved: if you are in company housing or a share house first, register that address now and file a change when you move — do not wait for the “real” apartment while the 14-day clock runs. And if a bank, employer, or carrier asks for your My Number before the notice arrives, register as early as you can and know that most procedures can proceed once you can present the notice or, later, the card.

FAQ

What if I landed at a small airport and have no residence card yet?

Register your address with your passport. The card is then mailed to your registered address by the Immigration Services Agency after registration. Until it arrives, your passport plus a certificate of residence covers most counter interactions.

Can I open a bank account or get a phone before registering my address?

In practice, no — and that is the point of the order. Banks want the residence card with the address printed on the back, and carriers verify that same card plus a payment method. Register first (Days 1–14), bank second, phone third, and use a data eSIM or rental Wi-Fi as a bridge in between.

Do students and dependents follow the same order?

The skeleton is identical: registration, NHI, pension. Students enroll in NHI and the National Pension themselves and can use the student special payment system for pension premiums instead of paying the full amount. If you want a part-time job as a student or dependent, you need permission to engage in activity other than that permitted under your status — it can be requested at the airport on arrival — with a standard limit of 28 hours per week.

What official mail should I expect, and can I ignore any of it?

Expect the Individual Number Notice (keep it safe), NHI premium bills, National Pension payment slips, your Certificate of Eligibility for NHI, and a My Number Card delivery postcard. Rule of thumb: no official envelope is ever junk. Open everything, and pay or file an exemption rather than ignoring a slip.
