The Dish You Didn’t Order at an Izakaya — Otoshi, Explained

It arrives unasked, it appears on the bill, and it is not a mistake. Here is what otoshi is, what it costs, and whether you can refuse it.

An izakaya (居酒屋) is Japan’s casual pub — small dishes, drinks, low tables or counter seats — and one of the best nights out in Japan. But it holds two surprises for first-timers. Surprise one: minutes after you sit down, a small dish arrives that nobody ordered. Surprise two: when the bill comes, there is a charge on it for every person at the table — for that dish.

Both surprises have the same name: otoshi (お通し). Once you know what it is, the confusion disappears and the charge stops feeling like a rip-off. This guide explains what otoshi is, what it typically costs, whether you can refuse it, what to say if you can’t eat it, and how to end the night smoothly — asking for the bill, splitting it the Japanese way, and knowing why you never tip.

The short answer

Otoshi is a small appetizer served automatically when you sit down. It works as the izakaya’s seat charge — think of it as a cover charge that comes with a snack. It typically costs around ¥300–¥500 per person, and at most places it is not optional. The smooth move is to treat it as “admission plus a first bite,” eat it, and enjoy it. In a group of four, expect roughly ¥1,200–¥2,000 of otoshi charges on the bill before a single drink is counted. When you want to leave, say “Okaikei onegai shimasu” (お会計お願いします — “The bill, please”) and the staff will take it from there. And: no tip, ever.

Step 1: Expect the little dish

Otoshi — called tsukidashi in western Japan — arrives before you order anything, often alongside your first drink order. It might be a small salad, simmered vegetables, or a bite of seafood. Whatever it is, it is not free, and it is not a mistake. It is the system working exactly as designed.

Why does it exist? Because in this style of pub, the seat itself is part of what you pay for. Rather than printing a bare “table charge” on the menu, the traditional izakaya folds that charge into a small dish the kitchen sends out immediately — something to eat while you look at the menu and wait for your first round. Every person at the table gets one, and every person is charged for one. Budget for it in advance and there is no sting at bill time: assuming otoshi is a free welcome snack is the single most common first-timer mistake.

A few honest exceptions, so you are not confused when you meet them: prices and house rules vary by shop, a few chains have no otoshi at all, and some places let you decline it. The pattern described here is the traditional one you will meet most often, especially at independent izakaya.

Step 2: Can you refuse it? The honest answer

Because otoshi doubles as the seat charge, most izakaya do not let you simply refuse it. Asking to have it removed just because you did not order it is a bit like asking a venue back home to waive its cover charge because you did not plan to stay long — the charge is a house rule, and arguing about it after the dish is on the table usually goes nowhere and sours an otherwise good night.

There is one real exception, and it is about what is served, not whether you pay. If you have an allergy or a religious dietary restriction, say so as you are being seated — many places will swap the dish for something you can eat. Timing is everything: asking before the dish comes out is a reasonable request; refusing it after it is on the table usually isn’t. The phrase you need is “Arerugī ga arimasu” (アレルギーがあります — “I have an allergy”), said early, ideally while pointing to the ingredient concerned if you know it.

If the price itself matters to you, it is also perfectly fine to ask in advance: “Otoshi wa ikura desu ka?” (お通しはいくらですか — “How much is the otoshi?”). Asking the price is normal; disputing the system at payment time is what creates the awkward scene travelers remember. If a no-otoshi venue matters more to you than the traditional atmosphere, choose one of the chains that does not serve it — that decision is made at the door, not at the register.

Step 3: Order in waves, the izakaya rhythm

The normal rhythm of an izakaya night is drinks first, then a few small dishes, then more as the table decides. You do not need to order everything at once — in fact, ordering in waves is the expected pattern, and dishes are sized for sharing and grazing rather than for one-plate-per-person dining. To call staff, say “Sumimasen” (すみません — “Excuse me”) or press the call button if your table has one. Nobody expects you to have the whole night planned in the first five minutes; the otoshi is partly there to cover exactly that unhurried start.

Step 4: Ask for the bill from your seat

When you are ready to go, do not wander around looking for a register. Start from your seat: catch a staff member’s eye and say “Okaikei onegai shimasu.” If words fail, crossing your two index fingers in an X shape is a widely understood gesture for “bill, please” in Japan — it works across the language barrier every time.

What happens next depends on the shop. Staff may bring the check to your table, take payment at the seat, or point you to the register. Follow their lead. One practical warning: small independent izakaya are sometimes cash only, so carry some yen even if the rest of your trip runs on cards. When the bill arrives, the otoshi line will be on it — per person, at the price the house charges — and now you know exactly what it is.

Step 5: Splitting the bill, Japanese style — and no tipping

Splitting evenly — warikan (割り勘) — is the default among friends and colleagues in Japan. The usual pattern: one person pays the whole bill, or everyone contributes their share in cash at the table. Restaurants generally don’t split a bill across multiple cards, so settle up among yourselves rather than asking the register to run four cards. Building the otoshi into everyone’s share is automatic, since it is charged per person anyway.

And there is no tipping. Not a small tip, not rounding up, not “keep the change for great service.” The otoshi and the listed prices already cover service. Leaving extra money will more likely confuse staff than please them — occasionally, someone will run after you down the street to return what you “forgot.” Paying the bill exactly, with a thank-you, is perfectly polite and exactly what is expected.

Before your izakaya night, run this quick checklist: add otoshi (about ¥300–¥500 per person) to your budget; if you have allergies, be ready to say so the moment you sit down; carry cash, because some small izakaya are cash-only; remember “Okaikei onegai shimasu”; and remember — no tip, paying exactly is polite.

FAQ

Is otoshi a scam aimed at tourists?

No. It is charged to everyone, Japanese customers included, per person, at traditional izakaya across the country — in western Japan under the name tsukidashi. It is the house’s seat charge served as food, which is why it appears whether or not you ordered anything yet.

Can I refuse the otoshi and have the charge removed?

At most places, no — because it doubles as the seat charge, it is not treated as an optional menu item. A few chains have no otoshi and some places let you decline it, but do not count on it. If you cannot eat the dish for allergy or religious reasons, say so when you are seated and many places will swap it for something you can eat.

How much should I budget for otoshi?

Typically around ¥300–¥500 per person. For a table of four, that is roughly ¥1,200–¥2,000 before drinks. If you want certainty, ask “Otoshi wa ikura desu ka?” when you sit down.

Should I tip if the service was excellent?

No. There is no tipping custom in Japan, and the otoshi and listed prices already cover service. Extra money left on the table is likely to be treated as money you forgot — staff may chase you to return it. A thank-you is the right gesture.
