Sick in Japan: What to Show at the Pharmacy and Say at the Clinic

You wake up in a hotel in Osaka or Kyoto with a fever and a drugstore shelf you cannot read. You do not need fluent Japanese — you need the right phrases to show, an understanding of the category printed on each box, and two phone numbers saved before you need them.

Getting sick abroad is disorienting in a specific way. At home you would know which aisle to walk down and when a symptom stops being a pharmacy problem and becomes a doctor problem. In Japan, all of that is behind a language barrier: the packaging is in Japanese, staff may not speak much English, and even explaining “I have diarrhea” out loud can feel impossible when you are already unwell.

This guide is deliberately narrow, and it is general information only: it does not diagnose anything and does not recommend any specific medicine. What it gives you is a working system — how to decide between a pharmacy, a clinic and an ambulance; phrases you can show on your phone; and a plain-English explanation of the legal category system printed on Japanese medicine boxes.

The short answer

  1. Mild, familiar symptoms and a pharmacy is open → go to a drugstore or pharmacy, show a symptom phrase to the pharmacist (薬剤師, yakuzaishi) or registered seller (登録販売者, tōroku hanbaisha), and let them guide you.
  2. Unsure, after hours, or you cannot communicate → call the JNTO Japan Visitor Hotline on 050-3816-2787. It runs 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, in English, Chinese, Korean and Japanese, and helps you find medical facilities that can assist in your language.
  3. Severe or alarming symptoms → call 119 for an ambulance. The call is free. Do not wait, and do not try to solve it at a drugstore.

Step 1: Decide — pharmacy, clinic, or 119?

Use this as a rough orientation, not a diagnosis. Call 119 now for severe or alarming problems — for example difficulty breathing, chest pain, loss of consciousness, signs of a stroke, heavy bleeding, a severe allergic reaction, or a child who is unresponsive or struggling to breathe. Ambulance and fire services are both reached on 119 in Japan.

See a doctor when symptoms are strong, persistent or getting worse, when they involve a young child, or when you are pregnant, have a chronic condition, or take regular medication. In Japan you generally start at a local clinic (クリニック, kurinikku) rather than a large hospital. A pharmacy first is reasonable for mild, familiar symptoms when you otherwise feel stable — and if the pharmacist thinks you need a doctor, take that advice.

One more number exists, with a caveat: in areas that offer it, #7119 is a consultation line staffed by medical professionals that advises whether to call an ambulance or visit a hospital — but availability varies by region, it is not offered everywhere, and the service is in Japanese. That is why the Visitor Hotline, 050-3816-2787 (from overseas: +81-50-3816-2787), run by the Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO), is usually the easier first call.

Step 2: At the pharmacy — show, don’t struggle

You do not need spoken Japanese. Show the relevant phrase on your phone screen, or point at it on paper. A shown sentence is understood instantly and cannot be misheard, and pharmacy staff in Japan deal with this daily. The core phrases are:

Add timing when you can: 昨日から (kinō kara, “since yesterday”). If you take regular medication, show a photo of your own medicine packaging — it helps the pharmacist avoid conflicts. Expect questions about age, other medicines, allergies and pregnancy before some sales: that is the system working as intended, not a refusal.

Step 3: Read the category printed on the box

Japanese over-the-counter medicines carry a legal risk category on the packaging, set under the system overseen by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW). It is not a strength score — it tells you how much caution and staff involvement the sale requires:

  • 要指導医薬品 (guidance-required medicines): sold only with face-to-face guidance from a pharmacist. You cannot simply take these off the shelf.
  • 第1類医薬品 (Class 1): higher-caution products only a pharmacist can sell, with information provided at the sale. Often kept behind the counter or in a locked case.
  • 指定第2類医薬品 (Designated Class 2): Class 2 products needing particular care — the “2” is shown in a frame or circle on the box. Ask staff about warnings and clashes with other medicines.
  • 第2類医薬品 (Class 2): many everyday cold, pain and stomach products. A pharmacist or a registered seller can sell them.
  • 第3類医薬品 (Class 3): the lowest-caution category; a pharmacist or registered seller can sell them.

The practical rule: the higher the caution, the more the conversation matters. For the guidance-required, Class 1 and Designated Class 2 groups, do not self-select in silence — use the “consult the pharmacist” phrase above. And never assume a box matches what you know from home: a medicine sold freely in your country may be restricted or differently dosed in Japan.

Step 4: At the clinic — the first five minutes

Bring your passport and a way to pay — clinics commonly expect payment on the day — and keep receipts for your travel insurer. At reception, show: 診察を受けたいです (Shinsatsu o uketai desu, “I would like to see a doctor”); 予約はありません (Yoyaku wa arimasen, “I don’t have an appointment”); 海外旅行保険に入っています (Kaigai ryokō hoken ni haitte imasu, “I have travel insurance”); and, before you leave, 領収書をください (Ryōshūsho o kudasai, “Please give me a receipt”).

You will usually fill in a short intake form — the symptom phrases in Step 2 work there too. If communication stalls, call the Visitor Hotline on 050-3816-2787 from the waiting room; helping visitors find suitable facilities is exactly what it is for.

FAQ

What number do I call in a medical emergency in Japan?

119, for both ambulance and fire services. The call is free. For help in English with finding care when it is not an emergency, call the JNTO Japan Visitor Hotline on 050-3816-2787, open 24 hours a day, 365 days a year.

Why won’t the staff just hand me the box I picked up?

Because of the category system. Guidance-required and Class 1 medicines can only be sold by a pharmacist, with guidance provided at the sale — the questions staff ask are part of that legal process.

Can I buy the same medicine I use at home?

Not necessarily. Availability, ingredients and doses differ between countries. Show the pharmacist your own packaging, or a photo of it, and let them advise you.

What should I prepare before I get sick?

Save 050-3816-2787 in your phone, keep photos of your regular medicines and allergy information on it, and carry your passport and a payment method.
